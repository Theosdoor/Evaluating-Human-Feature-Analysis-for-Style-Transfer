For our baseline, we use a pre-trained \texttt{horse2zebra} Contrastive Unpaired Translation (CUT) model \cite{park2020cut}, and fine-tune it on full frames from the training videos (1000 frames per domain; 2000 in total). The experimental setup is shown in Table \ref{tab:setup}. CUT uses multilayer, patchwise contrastive loss which is more effective than cycle consistency loss (e.g. CycleGAN \cite{CycleGAN2017, isola2017image}), and CUT has only one generator and one discriminator which makes it $\sim$50\% more efficient than CycleGAN \cite{park2020cut}.

Table \ref{tab:metrics} shows FID, KID \cite{parmar2021cleanfid} and LPIPS \cite{zhang2018perceptual} for both translation directions. Movie $\to$ game translation performs worse for FID (271.3 vs 239.4) and KID (0.105 vs 0.085), which suggests that the opposite direction may be easier to learn, but the better LPIPS score (0.286 vs 0.391) suggests that the model more aggressively restyles in the game $\to$ movie direction.

Figures \ref{fig:q21_failure} and \ref{fig:q21_success} show that game $\to$ movie transfer results in heavy blurring and a green tinge, which may be an artifact from the \texttt{horse2zebra} initialisation. However, movie $\to$ game style transfer sometimes works well (Figure \ref{fig:m2g_q21_success}) but occasionally fails or incorrectly distorts the image (Figure \ref{fig:msg_q21_failure}).

The pre-trained \texttt{horse2zebra} weights are tuned for animal textures not humans, which is a large domain gap. Results are plausible for 5 epochs of fine-tuning (no zebra stripes!), but longer fine-tuning on a more appropriate pre-trained base may improve results.

\subsection{Local (Temporal) Enhancement}

We improve the baseline in Section 2.1 using human feature analysis from Section 1 and temporal enhancements. 
We fine-tune the pre-trained \texttt{horse2zebra} CUT model on the patches from Section 1.3, using the same setup in Table \ref{tab:setup}.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Inference-time parameters specific to Section 2.2 (not covered by Table~\ref{tab:setup}).}
  \label{tab:q22_params}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    Parameter & Value & Role \\
    \midrule
    Blur threshold $\tau$     & 10                & Lenient floor for test patch acceptance \\
    Patch size                & $256\times256$    & Crop resize before CUT inference \\
    Feather kernel            & $21\times21$, $\sigma=5$ & Gaussian mask blended at bbox border \\
    EMA blend weight $\alpha$ & 0.3               & Temporal blend strength (0 = off) \\
    ST-GCN window $T$         & 5 frames          & Spatio-temporal classification window \\
    Excluded classes          & \texttt{others}   & GCN classes skipped during compositing \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

During test inference, we:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Extract human patches as in Section 1.1, except with more lenient $\tau=10$ and both $\tau_{sc}=0$ and $\delta_f=1$ to cover every test frame (Table \ref{tab:extract_params}).
  \item Classify patches using a spatio-temporal extension of the Section 1.2 GCN (ST-GCN, \cite{yan2018stgcn}) over a $T=5$ frame window per tracked person, and discard \texttt{Others} to reduce noise (Table \ref{tab:q22_params}).
  \item Apply the fine-tuned CUT model to the remaining patches.
  \item Resize and paste the translated patches back onto their respective frames. Use a $21\times21$ Gaussian kernel ($\sigma=5$) for feather blending to mitigate obvious seams.
  \item Apply exponential moving average (EMA) temporal blending ($\alpha=0.3$) with \texttt{cv2.addWeighted} \cite{opencv_library} between consecutive frames to reduce flicker.
\end{enumerate}

Table \ref{tab:metrics} shows that this method is an overall improvement, producing lower FID, KID and LPIPS scores for both directions. This implies that patch-level training significantly improves performance over full-frame training. Without feathering and EMA blending, the FID and KID were much higher because of obvious seams after pasting. These artifacts are still present in Figure \ref{fig:q22_compare}, so improved blending (e.g. pyramid blending) may further improve results.

However, Figure \ref{fig:m2g_q22_compare} shows that the enhanced method mostly failed to learn any movie $\to$ game transfer, and sometimes the baseline looks better. 
This may be because there are 3 different movies (compared to 1 game), and more epochs are needed to learn the distribution.
